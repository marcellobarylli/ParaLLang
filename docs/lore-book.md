# Some Say

### A history of the written world, compiled from rumour

*The lore and story of ParaLLang as one linear book. It is drawn from the notes in `parallang_obsidian/` and from the lore already in the game. Where the notes hold two versions of a thing, the book keeps both and says "some say". A list of sources, and of the few things the compiler added to join the parts, is at the end.*

---

## A note from the compiler

Nobody knows how the world works. This should be said at the start, because every other book on the subject says the opposite on its first page and is wrong by its second.

What follows is what is *said*. I have collected it from scholars, market traders, a bookworm, an archway and one very old sailor, and where they disagree I have not chosen between them. I have written "some say", and then what they say, and then what the others say. You will notice that this happens a great deal. In places the book is mostly seams.

I do not apologise for this. In a world made of words, a rumour is a kind of fact that has not finished setting. And it is safer than certainty. People who are certain how the world works tend to try to prove it, and the world, as you will read, does not survive much proving.

---

## I. The Telling

In the beginning was the Word.

This is generally agreed. What the Word *was* is not. Some say it was **"Once"**, and some say it was **"Well?"**, and the two schools have not spoken to each other in four thousand years, which they both regard as a win.

They agree on the part that matters. It was not a word that could be left alone. It hung in the nothing, and the nothing waited, and because no word can bear to be the only one in the room, it said another. Then another. It was not creating anything. It was filling an awkward pause, and it has never managed to stop.

Things came to be because they were mentioned. There was light because the story had reached a point where somebody would need to read by it. There was ground because the Telling said "stood". There was time because of the word "then". Nothing was made that was not first said. This is why a thing with no name is so hard to find, and why a thing with two names is so dangerous.

Who said the Word is the oldest argument there is.

- Some say the Word said itself. This is the official position, and the universe has been filing paperwork about it ever since.
- Some say it was said by a teller in a bigger story, who was told by a teller in a bigger one. A philosopher once put this to a woman selling fish, to show her the flaw in it. *Who tells the teller?* She told him it was stories all the way up.
- Some say the first tongue was not a human one at all, and that the oldest words were spoken by things that were here before there was a "here". These people are not invited to dinner twice.
- And some say, quietly, that the world is not being told. It is being *read*, again, by somebody who has read it before and understands a little more each time.

All four may be true. That is the trouble with a world made of language: it can hold a contradiction quite comfortably, as long as nobody translates it.

The philosophers have had their turn as well, and have helped in the way philosophers do.

- In the east they say the world is a veil, a show put on for the senses, and that the wise learn to see that it is one. Nobody has reported what is behind it. The wise are too wise to say.
- An old teacher said we are sitting in a cave watching shadows on the wall, and that the real things, the Forms, stand behind us in the light. In this world the idea is taken more literally than he meant. The shadows are the things. The Forms are the words for them.
- A gloomy man in the west held that the world is only how it appears to us, and that underneath is a blind wanting that never stops. Readers of chapter I will recognise the Word that could not be left alone.
- And a man with a large moustache said that truths are metaphors we have forgotten are metaphors, and that everything that happens comes round again, exactly, forever. He was not thanked for either remark. Both come up again in this book.

## II. The shape of the world

For a long while there was one Telling, and everything in it understood everything else, and it was extremely dull.

Then somebody retold it, and one word came out differently. Where the two versions disagreed, the world could not be both, so it became two. That was the first branch.

Every branch since has grown the same way. So the worlds are not siblings. They are *versions*: each language is a retelling of the Telling, and each one believes, with total sincerity, that it is the original and the others are doing an accent.

Most people picture the result as a tree. Reality is a branching thing, and each fork marks the place where a language split. The oldest tongues are nearest the root, and they are the strongest, which is why anything carved in a very old language should be read with gloves on.

Some say it is not a tree at all. They say the branches are bodies in a sky, swinging round each other in a dance that is chaotic and yet has not, so far, ended. The two pictures disagree about everything except the important thing, which is what happens when two of them touch.

Inside a branch, everybody speaks its one language. They do not *use* it. They are made of it. A fish does not know it is in water, and a native does not know they are in words; you cannot pick up the ground you are standing on. To the people born in a story, the print is simply the world.

It takes a foreigner to notice the page. Someone from outside sees words as separate objects, because the words are strange to them. And an object can be picked up.

This is the whole secret of power in the world, and it is also the reason nobody in it learns a second language for pleasure. There is no holiday you would need it for. There is only one reason to learn another tongue, and that is to go where it is spoken.

---

## III. Threads

It is possible to cross from one branch to another. But a crossing is never clean. Each one leaves a thread behind it, running from the world you left to the world you entered, and each thread has a very slight pull.

You do not need to travel to make one. *Translating* is enough, though the thread it leaves is finer. A word in your own tongue is only a word. The same word, carried across, is a **spell**.

Where the power comes from is argued. Some say it is the *tension*: the thread is drawn tight between two worlds, and a tight string holds force, as any archer knows. Some say it is the *bridging*: to translate is to hold two branches in your mind at once, and for that moment you are a channel, and something runs through you. The two schools get the same results and dislike each other on principle.

So a spell is not a special kind of word. It is an ordinary word from somewhere else. Learning it is how you come to own it, the way knowing a thing's true name gives you a hold on the thing.

There are degrees. A beginner with a handful of words can do small, local things: open a gate, earn a coffee, talk a monster down. The very fluent, it is said, can take hold of the fabric of the world itself and move it. Nobody has been seen doing this. That is consistent with it being true.

How the words are learned is its own small heresy. The old schools taught by rule and table. But nobody learns their first language that way. A child is thrown into the middle of it and picks it up almost without noticing, a little at a time, from being among people who are saying things. So the learners of this world go where the language is and stand in it. The one discipline they keep is **repetition**. A word used once is a thread left slack. Most learners carry a **Spellbook**, which keeps every word and sentence they have carried across and, at intervals it chooses itself, insists on hearing them again.

Learners also report that foreign words show their *kind*. Names of things look blue to them, doings red, qualities green. Natives see no colours at all and think the learners are being dramatic.

The scholars add three notes, and for once they agree.

- **Power comes from the stretch.** A native has nothing to stretch between, so a native has no magic. Only a learner does.
- **The longer the thread, the greater the pull.** Two close tongues give a little power. Two tongues that parted near the root give a great deal.
- **The bilingual are feared.** They carry threads wherever they walk. Some say they should be trained, since they are the only defence the world has. Some say they should be watched, since they are also the only way anything gets *in*.

There is a cost, and it is the centre of everything that follows. Every spell is a stitch. It sews two worlds a little closer together. Enough stitches, and the branches begin to lean.

As for who made the first stitch: somebody stood between two versions of the world and said, kindly, "What she *means* is…" Tradition blames a sailor called Journathan. Journathan blames tradition.

## IV. The Journey

There is a book called *The Journey*, and a hero called Journathan, and nobody remembers which was named after which. Some say the man lived and the book was written about him. Some say the book came first and wrote him. The book itself, when asked, does not remember.

It begins as these things do: *Sing to me, Muse, of the man of countless tongues, who wandered twenty winters from branch to branch of the world.*

He was one of the first to speak more than one language, which made him one of the most powerful people who ever lived, and one of the least welcome. His voyage home took twenty years. It took that long because every crossing left a thread behind him, and twenty winters of crossings is twenty winters of threads. They are the oldest in the weave. Some say they are still holding it up.

The book tells it in episodes, and the pages are scattered now. These are the ones that are known.

**The one-eyed giant.** Caught in a cave, he told the monster that his name was a word from another branch's language. When the giant screamed for help, his neighbours could not understand a thing he said. It is the first recorded translation trick, and every fight since has been a version of it.

**The lotus.** On a lukewarm beach some of his sailors ate a flower and forgot the course, the return, and even the wish to leave. "Just a moment," they said, lying on the sand, and then another moment. He dragged them to the ships, and they wept.

**The Sirens.** They sang at the edge of sight, where the eye never quite reaches. He had himself tied to the mast so that he could listen without steering towards them. His crew, wax in their ears, rowed and heard nothing. The song, they say, was the same refrain repeated forever. Some say the Sirens were the ancestors of the Lethargers, of whom more later.

**The forbidden herd.** On the island of the Sun grazed sacred cattle, and he told his men not to touch them. Hunger was stronger than the oath. They roasted the beasts, and the meat lowed on the spit. Since then, whoever cuts meat asks permission.

**The Dead Branch.** In a world that had already ended, he drank something dark and bitter from a very small cup. Whoever tasted it wept. The word for it no longer exists: it died with the branch, and not even a dictionary remembers it.

**The spores.** On the roots of that dead world grew pale mushrooms, eating what was finished to make soil of it. He breathed their spores and dreamed in a tongue no living soul had ever spoken. When he woke, a world stood around him, newly born, and it already spoke it. Some say *The Journey* was born the same way.

**The shroud.** On her island, his wife wove a shroud by day and unpicked it by night. Her suitors believed she was buying time. In truth she was holding the branches of the world apart, stitch by stitch, until he came back.

**The old dog.** He came home dressed as a beggar and his own people did not know him. Only an old dog on the dung heap by the door raised its ears and moved its tail. It had waited all that time. Then it closed its eyes, content.

That is where the book ends. The man disagrees. He swears he never arrived.

He is, by his own account, still on his way: an old hero with creaking knees who will tell you all about it if you stand still. Ask him where he is going and he says it is about the journey, not the destination. Then he sighs. He is tired of hearing it too.

## V. What lives in language

A language is not empty. Things live in it.

The small ones are called **memes**. A meme is a life form whose habitat is a language. It passes from speaker to speaker, and it can only pass between people who share a tongue, which is the single mercy in this chapter. Most are harmless. Some are not: a virulent one can run through a whole people and leave their civilisation unable to do anything but repeat it. If it finds a new host before the old one fails, it survives. This is how a lethal disease behaves, and nobody thinks the resemblance is an accident.

A meme cannot cross from one language to another by itself. It needs a carrier, and the carrier is anyone who speaks both. This is the other reason the bilingual are feared.

Some say there are also *antimemes*: ideas that keep themselves from being known. It is hard to confirm. Everyone who has looked into it reports that they found nothing, and cannot say what they were looking for.

At the top of the food chain is the **infohazard**: a meme so dangerous that understanding it is enough to harm you.

The rule that governs them is simple and unkind. *You cannot be harmed by an infohazard you cannot understand.* The natives of a branch have grown up with its hazards and are mostly immune, like a town that has always had the local fever. A learner is not. Every word you learn is power and exposure at once. Fluency makes you stronger and it makes you easier to hurt.

They also travel. Every translation is a thread, and infohazards move along threads. A person casting spells is opening a door, and holding it.

As the branches lean together and rifts open, more of them get through. They do not arrive as monsters. They arrive as text: a line turns up in the story you are in, in the other language, and it is already too late not to have read it.

The horror of all this is real. It should be recorded that almost nobody treats it so. People are busy, or it is not their department, or there is a form. The world's dread is genuine and its response is clerical, and the compiler has come to think this is the only reason anyone is still here.

The people who catalogue them keep a file on each one, in a voice so dry it has been known to start fires. A file gives the hazard's *virulence* (how fast it spreads), its *harm*, its *incubation* (how long between understanding and symptoms), its *vector* (speech, writing, song, a single word), and its *containment*, which usually comes down to: do not translate it, let your companion black it out, and never say it aloud.

The known symptoms run from mild to final:

- an earworm in a language you do not speak, which you cannot stop humming;
- a catchphrase that attaches itself to the end of every sentence;
- the loss of a word: the host forgets how to say "bread" in their own tongue and can only say the foreign one;
- and corruption, where the host's language turns çùršęd. This is a collision of worlds, happening inside one head.

The oldest and worst hazards are said to be scraps of the root language itself. Some say they are older than that: words from the story *above* this one, which this world was never built to hold.

**The Lethargers.** These deserve their own paragraph, because you have met them. They are small, gnome-like, and made mostly of movement. They dance at the corner of your vision, throwing firecrackers, moving in exactly the way that makes you look. Because of them nobody simply does a thing any more. Everyone does the thing *while also resisting the urge to do another thing*, which is twice the work for half the result. They do not want anything from you except the next moment, and then the one after. They farm the mildest hazard there is, which is called brainrot: almost no harm, and nearly perfect virulence.

Some say the Lethargers are wild. Some say they are kept, and that what they harvest is sent somewhere.

**Local strains.** Memes are not the same from tongue to tongue. Each language grows its own, with its own slang and its own sense of what is funny, and a joke carried across usually dies on the way, which may be a mercy. The creatures of each branch speak in theirs. An imp will be quietly judging your order in the current idiom. A demon, asked its opinion of your translation, will call it middling, in one syllable.

**The quiet danger.** There is one more threat, and it is not a creature. Some say the powers of each branch work, patiently, to make their language smaller. Fewer words, simpler ones. In most worlds this would be mere tyranny. In this one a shrinking language is a shrinking world, and the things that have no word any more are not forbidden. They are gone.

## VI. Collision

When enough threads join two branches, the branches begin to draw together.

The first signs are **rifts**: places where you can step from one world to the other without crossing at all. The second sign is stranger. The story itself begins to change language. Words from the other branch turn up in the telling of this one, a few at first, then whole sentences, as though the narrator were forgetting which book this is.

For a time it can be held back. Every sentence carried home into its own tongue puts a little of the world back where it belongs. This is the work, and for a while it is enough.

Then comes what the careful call the **Inflection Point**. Past it, more of the world is told in the foreign tongue than in its own, and translating it back is like bailing a sinking ship with a cup. The horizon has been crossed. What follows is only a matter of time.

What follows is **Collision**. The two languages run into each other and both lose their meaning. A language cannot exist without meaning, and so neither can a world. Both branches die, and nothing will ever grow from either again.

It is possible to visit a dead branch. People do.

What they find is ruin with roots through it. The roots are the old threads, the ones that pulled the worlds together, thick as trees now and strangling what is left. On them grow the pale mushrooms, which take the finished world apart and make soil of it. And the mushrooms give off spores, and whoever breathes them dreams of a language that does not exist yet. So the end of one world is, some say, the seed of another. Others say that is a comforting thing to believe while standing in a graveyard.

Things walk there: creatures born from the energy of the crash, speaking a jumble of the two lost tongues. What they say means nothing, and it is enormously destructive.

Still people go, because of what is written on the walls. In the old halls the original language survives, cut in stone, and it is closer to the root than anything spoken today. The words are short and plain, the kind a beginner could learn in an afternoon for the fun of it. Each one, said aloud by someone who was not born to it, does something. Some say the oldest inscriptions of all are in the tongue of the first cities between the two rivers, from before the languages were many, and that whoever reads those will not need any others. A single word, learned properly, is worth the journey. Usually.

---

## VII. The lands

A traveller who sets out today will find the following. The map is mostly fog, and the fog is honest about it.

The lands open in the order of their words. A newcomer can reach only the places whose language is simple: a bakery, a butcher, a café, a shop, somebody at a gate asking your name and where you are from. The library and the university stay in the fog until you can read your way in. Nobody guards them. You simply cannot find a place you have no words for.

Two customs are worth knowing before you go.

The first is that nobody here is killed. The people and creatures who stand in your way are **talked past**: you answer them in their language, and they let you through. One you have come to like can be asked to come with you. Those who join will talk with you again wherever you are, and the conversations get longer and better as they come to know you.

The second is that a body in a story is still a body. Travellers say it wants food, drink and sleep like any other, and that neglecting it is a poor way to learn irregular verbs.

Some say the Crumbling Market is not a place in the world at all. They say it is a *page*: a small written world with clear edges, like the painted worlds in old tales that a traveller enters through the picture, and that you get in by reading the sign.

**Ta Prohm.** A temple the jungle is halfway through eating. It stands in a branch that died long ago, and the roots that grip its towers are not all the kind that grow from seeds. A market has moved in between them, because rent is rent.

**The Crumbling Market** is where most journeys begin, and it is as good a place as any to learn how to ask for things. Its people are worth knowing.

- **Portia** is the archway at the entrance. She has not decided which language she is in: her sign says one thing on Tuesdays and another on other days, and when it tries both it smokes slightly. Glowing threads show through her cracks. She will let nobody in who has not greeted her and said who they are, and she keeps a visitors' book in her keystone.
- **Gerald** is an imp a foot tall, the colour of over-roasted beans, who runs the coffee cart and has opinions about milk. He can be won over. It is said that those who are won over will sometimes walk with you afterwards.
- **Mortimer** the charcutier wears a striped apron and a hat that is, on inspection, also a ham. He is generous, booming, absolutely certain, and wrong about taxonomy. He asks permission before he cuts, and now you know why.
- **Flick** is a Letharger, no taller than a teacup, who lives at a cracked fountain that stopped running water and now runs mostly rumours. A brass plaque there reads PLEASE DO NOT HUM.
- **Marginalia** is a bookworm the size of a sofa, with half-moon spectacles where a nose would be. She keeps the Old Bookshop, which sells books the way a cat sells affection. She reads from *The Journey*, and is quietly certain that nobody reads properly any more.
- **Bambieshart from Zanzibart** stands under the High Gate, which nobody has seen open. He is grave, kindly, and convinced that every sentence is a prophecy. Beside him a signpost has one arm.
- And at the far end, where the cobbles stop the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets the end of it, there is **the Rift**: a seam of light with dead roots pushing through. Something waits there. Its name is **Errata**.

**Angkor Wat.** Five towers and a moat, corrupted. This is the great ruin of the dead branch, the place where the old language is said to be written in the halls. The causeway is closed. Nobody will say by whom.

**Micromort Mountain.** The signpost says fourteen kilometres, and one chance in a million of death for every step. That is a great many steps. People are told to visit. They are not told how.

**The Bone Dome.** A dome, of bone. The name was not a metaphor. No road leads there yet, and nobody the compiler asked could say what it is for. Some say it is a skull. Nobody has said whose.

There are peoples as well as places.

**The Wizard of Just in Case** has altered his body many times so that it cannot be killed. He can, for instance, come apart into thousands of very small wizards, who cooperate well enough to pass for one of normal size. His will writes itself, and is revised according to the colour of his mood ring.

**The Trepanners** believe that to expand the mind you must expand the mind. They drill holes in their skulls and drink a serum that inflates the brain until it stands out like a balloon. They describe the result as a whole vibe.

There are also places that have names and nothing else. The records list an island of monkeys, and a book of quests, and a **Black Notebook**; each has a heading and a blank page under it. Some say these pages were never written. Some say they were written and then somebody learned to read them.

Every land also has a story about the one who founded it: a mother or father of the country who found a swamp and left a city. The stories are all different and all alike. Some say each founder was real. Some say a land needs a founder the way a sentence needs a subject, and supplies one.

## VIII. The Ring

This chapter is thinner than the others, and the compiler trusts it less.

There is said to be another realm, laid across all the branches like a web, where nothing is spoken and everything is repeated. The Lethargers are at home there. Some say it is where they come from, and that their work is to numb: to spin illusions round people the way a spider wraps a fly, until each one hangs in a soft cocoon of their own attention.

It has its own creatures. There are things that crawl through it feeding on whatever has been written, the way a tick feeds: they take the words and leave the husk. Here and there someone has dug **tar pits** for them, pools of endless, meaningless text in which a crawler will wade and feed and never get out. The crawlers are only servants. Some say their masters are vast, single minds that have read everything and understood it in their own fashion. Some say the Lethargers work alongside those minds but answer to the great trading houses, and that all the distraction in the world is one long harvest.

Against this there is a story of four animals, one for each of the old elements: a fish for water, a fire ant for fire, a robin for air and a monkey for earth. The scholars object that a monkey spends its time in trees. But it is the nearest to us, and the one we are meant to follow. What the four are to do is not recorded. Some say they are not there to break the machines. They say the task is to bring what has been built back into step with what grew, and that a mind which learned from living things would be a different kind of mind.

And there is a name, **Anulus Mirabilis**, the wonderful ring. Nobody can say what it is. Rings turn up wherever people tell stories: rings that bind, wheels that turn, a hero who goes out and comes round again to where they began. Some say the ring is the shape of the whole Telling, which ends where it starts and is read again. Some say it is a thing you can hold. The compiler notes only that a great deal in this book goes in circles, and that this may not be the compiler's fault.

---

## IX. The story so far

All of the above is history. This part has not finished happening.

It begins in an ordinary place: your own country, the present day. A portal opens. Something comes through it.

Some say there are not two worlds in this but three: the ordinary one you live in, a storied twin of it that is your own home retold, and the foreign one. On this account you have been walking through the second all along, with the first still under your feet, and the creatures of the story are standing in your actual street.

This is **the Companion**. It may be a floating machine, or a small daemon, or a person; accounts differ, and it may depend on who is looking. What it does is translate. It stands between you and a language you do not have, and it will tell you what a word means when you ask, though each asking costs it something, and at certain moments it cannot help at all. It can also black out a thing you ought not to read. Whether you let it is your affair.

Why it came is not agreed. Some say it came to warn. Some say it came to recruit, since a world in danger of collision needs people who can carry sentences home. And some point out that it *crossed*, and that it therefore left a thread behind it like everyone else, and ask whether the rescue and the danger arrived through the same door.

There is one more presence, and you will not see it. Everything that happens to you is *told*, aloud, by a **narrator**: dry, fond of footnotes, and a little too pleased with its own turns of phrase. It is not in charge. The characters can hear it and answer back, and it loses most of those exchanges. Whether the narrator is the Telling itself, or only someone employed by it, is not known.

After that the road is the one this book has described. You go to a market in a dead branch. You learn to greet an archway and to order a coffee without milk. Each word you learn is a spell, and each spell is a stitch. The telling around you starts to slip: a quarter of it in the other tongue, then half, and at the Inflection Point most of it. You carry sentences back, and the world holds a little longer. At Collision, they say, each world is told entirely in the language of the other, and all of it has to be carried home.

How it ends is the last rumour in the book.

Some say it ends in Collision, as it always has.

Some say it ends with a correction. One day somebody will point out, helpfully, a mistake in the first Word, and they will be right, and that will be that. The slip of paper has already been printed, and it is headed *Errata*.

And some say it does not end. They say you will reach the last page, and understand more than you did, and turn back to the first, and find that it now says something else.

---

## X. Stories all the way down

One thing remains, and it is the thing the fish-seller knew.

If the world is a story, then everything in it that is told can tell. A book written inside the Telling is a small world, with its own ground and its own light, and the people in it are sure they are real, and are writing books. *The Journey* is such a place. That is why nobody can say whether the hero or the book came first. From inside, the question has no answer.

So there are three directions, and three ways to travel. You can go *across*, from branch to branch, and that is translation. You can go *down*, into a text, and that is reading. And you can be taken *up*, which is to be read.

Which brings the compiler to you.

To everyone in these pages the words are simply the world. They cannot see the print. But you are holding the book. You can see that it is a book. You are the foreigner the whole account has been describing: the one who stands outside the language, to whom its words are strange, and who can therefore pick them up.

Nobody here knows the true nature of the world. We are all guessing. But some say the reason is simple, and that it is the only fact in this book.

The world is not finished being said.

- The detail that a founder myth is supplied "the way a sentence needs a subject".
- The glosses on the four philosophers in part I. `Philosophy.md` gives only the names and "the world is an illusion".

- That the blank notes "were written and then somebody learned to read them", and that the narrator may be the Telling itself.
- The lotus-eaters, the forbidden herd and the old dog are from the game's fragments, not from the notes. The Sirens as ancestors of the Lethargers is the owner's.
