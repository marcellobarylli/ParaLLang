- Language itself underpins reality. Meta-narrative: The world is written. Since the user interacts with the world through language, similarly to how one reads a book, they are interacting with the reality of the world itself. This is why words have power over the world. This manifests at multiple levels: weak users can use words to make local effects, such as spells. Strong users can manipulate the fabric of reality itself. Spells are central: A spell is just a word / sentence but in the user's foreign language. Learning this word means obtaining its power, similar to how learning the true name of something unlocks its power in Name of the Wind (Rothfuss)
- Reality is a branching structure, where each branch points marks the split point of a language. It is possible to travel between branches (interdimensional travel), but each time one does so, a little "string" is left behind that connects the two worlds. Each string has a weak pull. If enough travels occur, these strings will begin to pull together the branches, initiating a collision. 
	- Not only does a travel between dimensions cause a string, but translating alone does too (smaller strings). This is why translation is the source of a spell's power, not the word on its own. The 'tension' (again, string metaphor) is what generates power.
	- Or: When you translate, you hold two branches in mind at once, and that act of bridging is what channels energy through the "strings" between worlds. Natives have no second frame of reference, so they have nothing to bridge to. This would also explain why bilingual characters are feared (they're the ones with real power), and it ties directly into the meme-vector lore where bilinguals are dangerous conduits.
	- **The fish doesn't know it's water.** Natives are _constituted by_ their language — they don't wield it, they _are_ it. You can't pick up the ground you're standing on. A foreign learner, by contrast, stands outside the linguistic substrate. They perceive words as discrete objects precisely because those words are alien to them, and that perceptual distance is what allows words to be grasped as tools. This dovetails with the 4th wall conceit: the player is literally a reader engaging with text-as-reality from outside it. Natives are characters in the book. The player is the one holding the book.
	- If bridging is what channels the energy through the strings, then a defense strategy is to train multilingual users. The power that can be drawn through the strings scales with the string's length: Eg english to spanish will yield more power than spanish to italian, but less than english to chinese. This language branching structure (as it actually historically happened in our world) mirrors the branching structure of the fictional universe. Furthermore, bilingual characters are different from the ones in our real world: Here, we learn languages because we might travel or live in a different country. In the fictional one, every human speaks the same language by default (lore weakpoint). The only reason to learn another language is for multidimensional purposes.  
	- I love the string metaphor. It goes a long way: Every time we cast a spell, we are literally performing another '*stitch*' in the '*fabric*' of reality (sewing the 2 universes together). This stitching is dangerous, as its eventual end is always collision. Here a 'gravitational' perspective is mixed with a sewing perspective, but that's cool.  
	- I am still a bit hazy on the universes history though. What could be the generating process behind branching? In our real world, language undergoes a sort of evolution. Eg from proto-indoeuropean. This is spatiotemporally defined as peoples moved across land / sea and time. However, what could a sound explanation be in our fictional universe? We can think of these 2 forces: for a given point in the branching structure: the **origins of the branch**, and the '**first stitch**' that acts as a counterforce
- As the branches approach each other, rifts begin appearing that allow travel between the 2 worlds without jumping. In a 4th wall break, the narrative itself starts to incorporate elements of the respectively other language. If the collision occurs, both languages become corrupted and lose their meaning. Chaos is born. Both respective branches die, since language requires meaning to exist, thus reality does too. Once a language loses its meaning, its respective reality dies, preventing future branches.
- It is possible to visit such dead branches. One example is of an ancient civilisation ([[LOCATIONS#Angkor Wat Ancient Temples, Corrupted|Angkor wat with roots growing amongst the temple ruins]])). The roots represent the strings pulling the worlds together, now strangling the land. 
- Corrupted beings of chaos roam these lands, spawned from the energy released by the collision. They speak the jumbled words of the 2 languages, which contain immense destructive power. It is worth visiting these places, since the original languages can be found in ancient halls and inscribed in stone. These languages hold immense power, since they are closer to the root of the cosmic tree. Maybe throw in some Mesopotamian lore a la 'Snow Crash' or see if there's Proto-Indoeuropean sources. Like Skyrim, learning the shouts of power: They can be simple words for players to learn just for fun, but have in-game benefits.

### memes
Memes are like life forms that exist within a language. They can spread from one language to the next. There are highly virulent memes that can affect a population of language speakers, eventually rendering their civilisation incompetent and leading to its demise. However, if it can jump to another host in time, the meme, or collection of memes, will persist. Akin to a lethal virus
- language memes can only be spread between people who speak the same language (obviously). However, bilinguals act as vectors for the memes to spread across languages

#### Infohazards
*Classic (Lovecraft + Pratchett) × modern (SCP), like [[GAME MECHANICS#Terry Pratchett × meme culture, absurd humour|Pratchett × meme culture]] for the tone.*

An infohazard is a meme so dangerous that understanding it is enough to harm you. It is the predator at the top of the [[#memes|meme]] food chain.
- **Classic (Lovecraft):** forbidden knowledge. The text that drives the reader mad, the name that should not be spoken, the cosmic horror of finding out what is behind the page. Lovecraft's horror is already linguistic: the danger is in *reading* it
- **Classic, played for laughs (Pratchett):** the same dread but absurd. The Necrotelicomnicon, his Necronomicon parody, kept in the Unseen University Library. The Things from the Dungeon Dimensions, crowding at the edges of reality wherever magic wears it thin. The horror is real, but everyone is too busy, too bureaucratic or too stupid to take it seriously. This is [[GAME MECHANICS#Terry Pratchett × meme culture, absurd humour|our tone]]
- **Modern (SCP):** we only take infohazards from SCP culture. What we borrow is the clinical, deadpan catalogue: every hazard has a file, properties and containment procedures, written in a voice so dry it becomes funny
- **Infohazards are native to the foreign realm.** They live in the exo-language and only harm someone who understands it
	- The fish does not know it is water: natives of that realm are constituted by their language and have grown up with its infohazards, like a population immune to its local diseases. The player is not. The same distance that lets a learner grasp words as tools also exposes them
	- The core tension: **you can't be harmed by an infohazard you can't understand.** Every word you learn is power and exposure at once. Fluency makes you stronger and more vulnerable
- **They breach through.** Every translation is a string, and infohazards travel along strings. The player's own spellcasting is a breach vector, and bilinguals are the carriers (see the [[#memes|meme vectors]]). As the branches approach and rifts open, more of them leak into the native realm's narrative ([[#4th wall|4th wall]]: they show up in the text the player is reading)
- **Properties (the SCP-style file):**
	- *Virulence:* how fast it spreads from host to host
	- *Harm:* what it does to the host, from annoying to lethal
	- *Incubation:* how long between understanding it and the symptoms
	- *Vector:* speech, writing, song, a single word, a full sentence
	- *Containment:* how you keep it from spreading (don't translate it, let the [[CHARACTERS#The Companion|companion]] redact it, never say it out loud)
- **Symptom ideas, from mild to lethal:**
	- An earworm: you can't stop humming a song in the exo-language
	- A catchphrase that attaches to the end of every sentence the host says
	- Involuntary code-switching: the host forgets the native word for 'bread' and can only say the foreign one
	- Corruption: the host's native language turns çùršęd. At scale, this is a small collision in a single mind
- **Gameplay hooks:**
	- [[GAME MECHANICS|Dark Souls-style]] optional texts: some untranslated passages are infohazards. Translating them gives juicy lore, but at a cost
	- The [[CHARACTERS#The Companion|companion]] can redact or blur a hazard, but it is limited during certain events, like everything else it does
	- The [[GAME MECHANICS#Option A|Spellbook]] keeps a containment file for every infohazard you encounter, written in the deadpan SCP voice
	- The [[CHARACTERS#The Lethargers|Lethargers]] farm the milder strains: brainrot is an infohazard with low harm and very high virulence
- This could also absorb the eldritch-gods idea instead of adding another influence: the oldest and most dangerous infohazards are fragments of the [[#4th wall|eldritch root language]], closest to the root of the cosmic tree
